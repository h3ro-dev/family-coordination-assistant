Looheru AI Voice Calling Legal and Product Risk Research

Scope and attached-context findings

I reviewed the attached Looheru bundle first. The current repo and docs show a deliberately narrow voice architecture rather than a fully autonomous realtime agent: outbound voice jobs, TwiML <Gather input="speech">, rule-based parsing for slot extraction and yes/no confirmation, a provider-agnostic /webhooks/voice/result, and durable workflow state. The main product-risk issue in the current implementation is that it already captures and stores call-content artifacts: speech-to-text results are written into voice-job/debug fields and message-event logs, and the pilot materials still have some documentation drift about whether Phase 1 “places phone calls yet.” That means the legal risk is not only “future AI voice” risk; the current design already creates call-content retention, consent, and healthcare-boundary questions.

Primary sources used for current law and guidance include urlFCC AI-voice declaratory rulingturn0search4 (Feb. 8, 2024), urlFTC telemarketing guidanceturn13search0 (FTC guidance page currently available as of May 11, 2026), urlUtah Code Chapter 77turn18search0 (effective May 7, 2025), urlCalifornia Health and Safety Code § 1339.75turn26search0 (effective Jan. 1, 2025), urlHHS personal-representatives guidanceturn1search7 (Jan. 5, 2024), urlHHS health-app resourcesturn2search6 (updated Apr. 22, 2026), urlTwilio recording guidanceturn3search1, and urlTwilio HIPAA guidanceturn4search0.

Core legal conclusion

The parent-authorized, task-specific nature of the call helps a lot on the telemarketing/solicitation question, but it does not remove the core legal issues created by artificial voice, recording/transcription, identity/authority verification, and health-data handling. In other words: this is usually not classic outbound marketing, but it can still be a robocall for TCPA purposes if an AI-generated voice is used, and it can still trigger multi-state recording/interception rules if the system records or transcribes the office’s speech. citeturn15view5turn15view4turn15view1turn15view18turn15view19turn15view20

The safest high-confidence launch posture is therefore: keep the first wedge nonclinical, use voice only for published business main lines or verified landlines, disclose immediately that the caller is an automated assistant acting for a parent, do not record audio in MVP, minimize or eliminate raw transcripts, and keep healthcare/therapy/special-needs scheduling in a human-assisted or human-reviewed lane until counsel confirms state-by-state rules and proof-of-authority workflows. That conclusion follows from the combined effect of FCC AI-voice treatment, FTC telemarketing scope, state recording laws, and HHS guidance on provider verification and personal representatives. citeturn15view1turn15view5turn15view8turn15view6turn15view7turn15view17

Federal legal baseline

Under the entity["organization","Federal Communications Commission","U.S. telecommunications regulator"]’s February 2024 declaratory ruling, AI-generated human-sounding voice calls fall within the TCPA’s existing restrictions on calls using an “artificial or prerecorded voice.” The FCC materials and TCPA text distinguish sharply between calls to wireless numbers and calls to residential lines: absent an exception, calls using an artificial or prerecorded voice to wireless numbers require the called party’s prior express consent, while the residential-line rules focus on residential subscribers and exemptions for certain classes of calls. The FCC also continues to codify exemptions for certain wireless calls by healthcare providers, package delivery companies, and financial institutions, but those are narrow and do not create a general carveout for a consumer family-assistant product. citeturn15view1turn15view2turn28view0turn28view2turn28view1

That creates an important product distinction. If Looheru calls a business landline or main office number, federal TCPA exposure is materially lower than if it calls a business mobile number, a receptionist’s cell, or an otherwise wireless/charged service. Parent authorization does not substitute for the called office’s consent on a wireless line, because the TCPA consent framework is tied to the “called party.” So the parent’s permission helps establish agency and purpose, but not universal telecom consent. This is one of the most important MVP design implications from the federal layer. citeturn15view1turn15view2

Under the entity["organization","Federal Trade Commission","U.S. consumer protection agency"]’s Telemarketing Sales Rule, the call purpose matters. The TSR applies to calls made as part of a campaign to induce the purchase of goods or services, and FTC guidance expressly says the rule does not apply to purely informational messages that are not delivered to induce purchases. FTC guidance also says that most business-to-business calls are exempt from the TSR, subject to specific exceptions. That means a parent-authorized scheduling or availability-check call to a business is much less likely to be treated as telemarketing than a sales, lead-generation, subscription-upgrade, or promotional workflow. citeturn15view5turn15view4turn13search1

The federal authorities I reviewed do not establish a universal, across-the-board federal rule that every non-telemarketing AI voice call must identify itself as AI at the start. But the safer compliance reading is still to disclose it immediately, because the legal frameworks you do have are converging on transparency: Utah’s live disclosure law, California’s healthcare AI disclosure law, state recording-notice rules, and ordinary unfair/deceptive-practices risk if the system sounds human and does not identify itself as automated. That is why “disclose first” is the right product rule even where a specific federal disclosure mandate is not obvious. citeturn19view0turn26search0turn15view12

State law map

For AI disclosure, the clearest live state law I found is in entity["state","Utah","Utah, USA"]. Utah Code Chapter 77 now requires a supplier using generative AI in a consumer transaction to disclose that the user is interacting with AI if the individual clearly asks, and it imposes stronger requirements for “regulated occupations” involving high-risk AI interactions. Utah also creates a safe harbor if the system clearly and conspicuously discloses at the outset and throughout the interaction that it is generative AI, not human, or an AI assistant. For oral interactions requiring disclosure, the statute says disclosure must be verbal at the start. citeturn19view0turn19view1

In entity["state","California","California, USA"], the older bot law is real but narrow: it covers online bots used to mislead someone in order to influence a commercial transaction or vote, not ordinary phone calls. California also now has a healthcare AI disclosure law, Health and Safety Code § 1339.75, but it applies to a health facility, clinic, physician’s office, or group practice that uses generative AI to generate patient communications about patient clinical information. Critically for your use case, the statute says “patient clinical information” does not include appointment scheduling, billing, or other clerical/business matters. So this law is highly relevant if Looheru later sells into provider-to-patient clinical communications, but it does not squarely regulate a parent’s AI assistant calling an office just to ask about administrative availability. citeturn20view0turn26search0turn26search1

In entity["state","Colorado","Colorado, USA"], the enacted AI Act is aimed at high-risk AI systems used in consequential decisions, and its effective date was pushed to June 30, 2026. Based on the bill materials, it is about algorithmic discrimination in consequential decisions, not routine scheduling logistics. That means it is not the primary law governing an MVP availability-calling assistant, though it could become relevant later if the system is used inside healthcare access, triage, or eligibility decisions. citeturn15view13turn15view14

For call recording / interception / transcription, the high-risk states for phone calls are the ones commonly treated as all-party or notice-first jurisdictions. In the sources reviewed, that list includes entity["state","California","California, USA"], entity["state","Florida","Florida, USA"], entity["state","Illinois","Illinois, USA"], entity["state","Maryland","Maryland, USA"], entity["state","Massachusetts","Massachusetts, USA"], entity["state","Montana","Montana, USA"], entity["state","Nevada","Nevada, USA"] for phone calls, entity["state","New Hampshire","New Hampshire, USA"], entity["state","Pennsylvania","Pennsylvania, USA"], and entity["state","Washington","Washington, USA"], with entity["state","Connecticut","Connecticut, USA"] adding a notable civil-risk overlay and entity["state","Delaware","Delaware, USA"] better treated as high-risk because of interpretive uncertainty. Official examples include entity["state","Florida","Florida, USA"]’s statute requiring all parties’ prior consent, entity["state","Washington","Washington, USA"]’s rule that notice can satisfy all-party consent when announced effectively and recorded, and entity["state","Pennsylvania","Pennsylvania, USA"]’s exception where all parties have given prior consent. citeturn16view0turn16view1turn16view3turn16view4turn16view5turn16view7turn16view8turn16view9turn16view10turn15view18turn15view19turn15view20

A practical but important inference follows from those laws: even if you do not keep mp3 files, speech-to-text capture and stored transcripts are risky enough that you should treat them as a recording/interception issue unless counsel says otherwise. urlTwilio’s own recording docsturn3search1 explicitly warn that if you record calls, you must comply with consent laws. Your current repo design already stores speech-derived text, so from a risk perspective the MVP should behave as though “transcription is regulated content capture,” not as though “no audio file means no consent issue.” citeturn15view12turn15view17turn15view18turn15view19turn15view20

Healthcare and HIPAA boundary

The biggest boundary question is whether Looheru is a family logistics product or part of a provider’s HIPAA workflow. The entity["organization","U.S. Department of Health and Human Services","federal health department"]’s app guidance and business-associate guidance support a key distinction: a consumer-selected health app generally is not automatically a HIPAA business associate merely because it handles health-related information; it becomes a business associate when it is creating, receiving, maintaining, or transmitting PHI on behalf of a covered entity or as part of covered-entity services. So if a parent independently uses Looheru to coordinate family logistics, that is generally a consumer-side product question, not automatically a HIPAA-covered workflow. If Looheru later integrates with a provider, sends patient outreach for a clinic, works inside a patient portal, or is contracted by the provider to handle scheduling, then the HIPAA analysis changes substantially. citeturn1search8turn2search3turn15view11turn1search9

That does not mean the data is unregulated. HHS and the FTC both emphasize that consumer health information can still trigger privacy and security duties outside HIPAA, and the FTC Health Breach Notification Rule applies to covered personal health record vendors and related entities handling unsecured health information. So “not a business associate yet” is not the same thing as “no health-data compliance problem.” It just means the legal regime is more likely to be FTC/UDAP/consumer-health-data risk than full HIPAA program obligations at the earliest stage. citeturn15view9turn15view10turn2search8turn2search19

When the call is to a healthcare provider, the parent’s status matters. HHS says a personal representative stands in the shoes of the individual for HIPAA purposes, and a parent is usually the personal representative for a minor child. But HHS also says there are important exceptions, including situations where state law lets the minor consent to the service, someone else is legally authorized to consent, or the parent agreed to a confidential provider relationship. HHS also says providers must verify the identity and authority of a requester if they are not known to the provider. That means a receptionist may be legally entitled to refuse to discuss details with an AI system unless the office recognizes the parent or has documented authorization on file. citeturn15view6turn15view7turn15view8turn1search6

This is why therapy, behavioral health, and special-needs scheduling sit in a different risk band from camp or dentist scheduling. The underlying task may look “administrative,” but the conversation often drifts into diagnosis, treatment frequency, disability accommodations, mental-health context, insurance handling, or teen confidentiality. Even when the app remains consumer-side, those calls move quickly from “calendar logistics” into sensitive-health-data territory. In addition, if Looheru ever becomes a provider-side tool, certain state/provider AI disclosure laws become directly relevant. citeturn15view7turn19view0turn26search0

MVP guardrails and workflow classification

Launch-safe recommendations

The safest launch path is to make voice MVP = nonclinical business coordination only. Camps, schools, daycares, tutors, classes, household service providers, sitter agencies, and other nonclinical offices are the best fit. Even there, the system should call only a published business main number or a number verified as non-wireless, immediately identify itself as automated, avoid recording, and give the office an easy path to request human callback or permanent opt-out. citeturn15view1turn15view5turn19view0

For healthcare offices, the launch-safe approach is human-assisted, not fully autonomous. Let the system: prepare the call, surface the parent’s task, collect the office number, maybe place/connect the call, and capture structured slot results after a human or authorized office interaction. Do not let the MVP freely converse with clinics, dentists, therapists, or caregivers about anything beyond narrow logistics until counsel signs off on authority handling, recording/transcription treatment, and state launch rules. citeturn15view8turn15view6turn15view7

Exact product guardrails for the MVP

These should be product requirements, not “best-effort” policies:

  • Only call published business main numbers or verified landlines. Block or require human approval for any number identified as wireless, personal mobile, or unknown line type. The federal reason is that AI-generated voice remains regulated as artificial voice, and wireless-number consent is the hard edge. citeturn15view1turn15view2
  • No audio recording in MVP. Disable deliberate call recording. If speech recognition/transcription is needed, treat it as regulated content capture and either block it in all-party/high-risk states or obtain clear notice/consent before continuing. citeturn15view12turn15view17turn15view18turn15view19turn15view20
  • No raw transcript retention by default. Store structured outcomes only: reached/not reached, office callback number, offered slots, booking-confirmed yes/no, escalation reason, and opt-out. If temporary transcript text is used for slot extraction, delete it immediately after normalization unless counsel specifically approves retention.
  • No clinical content, no insurance, no DOB/SSN/MRN collection. The system should refuse and escalate if asked for symptoms, diagnosis, medication, referral details, treatment history, mental-health details, insurance/member ID, date of birth, Social Security number, medical record number, or disability narrative.
  • No voicemail drop. If the office does not answer, do not leave an AI voicemail in MVP. Queue human callback or approved alternate channel.
  • No pretending to be human and no pretending to be the provider. The system may say it is calling on behalf of the parent, but it should never imply it is the parent, the clinic, a nurse, or a licensed professional.
  • Easy office opt-out. Every call flow should support immediate “please have the parent call us directly” and a durable do-not-auto-call flag for that office.
  • Human takeover triggers must be deterministic. Trigger human review if the office asks for identity verification beyond name/relationship, mentions confidentiality restrictions, requests insurance/clinical details, asks to discuss treatment, or sounds uncertain about speaking with AI.

What data should not be stored in early pilots

Do not store the following in MVP unless and until the product has a formally reviewed health-data program: call audio; full office speech transcripts; diagnosis or reason-for-visit; symptoms; medications; insurance/member IDs; medical-record numbers; dates of birth; Social Security numbers; therapy notes; mental-health details; special-needs descriptions or accommodation narratives; and child-identifying health context beyond the minimum needed to request scheduling. This is not because every item is automatically HIPAA-regulated in consumer mode, but because they push the system toward PHI-like handling, FTC health-data risk, and high-sensitivity breach exposure with very little MVP benefit. citeturn15view9turn15view10turn2search13

How deterministic logic should reduce risk

The current attached architecture is moving in the right direction. Keep that direction and make it stricter. The voice agent should have a very small allowed action set: identify itself, state who authorized the call, ask for next available slots, confirm a specific slot, ask for a callback number, and end the call. It should not improvise, negotiate, answer substantive questions, interpret medical instructions, or infer family facts. If the answer does not fit the schema, it should stop and escalate. This is both a product-reliability benefit and a legal-risk reduction strategy because it reduces the chance that the assistant drifts into misrepresentation, unreviewed health-data collection, or clinical communication.

Red, yellow, and green workflow classification

Green

  • Camps, classes, tutors, daycares, schools, sitter agencies, nonclinical caregivers, household services.
  • Questions limited to availability, business hours, whether they are accepting bookings, and how to book.
  • No recording, no transcript retention, no child health details.

Yellow

  • Dentists, pediatric offices, general medical clinics, therapists, home-health or special-needs service coordinators.
  • Only administrative scheduling, callback requests, or confirmation of a parent-selected time.
  • Human pre-approval, stronger logging, number verification, and hard stop on clinical/insurance/disability details. Parent may need to take over live.

Red

  • Any clinical triage, diagnosis, treatment discussion, medication refill, referral management, prior authorization, billing dispute, insurance benefits call, behavioral-health content, teen-confidentiality scenario, substance-use treatment context, or provider-integrated patient workflow.
  • Any call involving audio recording, durable transcript retention, cloned or human-sounding deceptive voice, or outbound calls to mobile numbers without recipient consent.
  • Any workflow where the system must prove patient identity or authority beyond simple parent/child identification.

Required counsel review questions and exact language candidates

Required counsel review questions

Counsel should answer these before any autonomous healthcare-adjacent voice launch:

  • Whether an AI-generated interactive voice on a business mobile number should be treated as requiring the called party’s prior express consent under the TCPA, even when the call is parent-authorized and purely logistical.
  • Whether speech-to-text capture without saved audio is best treated as “recording” or “interception” under the launch states’ statutes.
  • Which launch states are acceptable for an MVP with no recording but temporary STT capture, and which states should be blocked unless a human places the call.
  • What proof of authority a provider may reasonably require when an AI assistant calls on behalf of a parent.
  • How minor-consent exceptions, especially for adolescent mental-health and related services, change the provider-call script.
  • Whether Looheru should use separate scripts and disclosures for healthcare vs nonhealthcare offices.
  • At what point provider-side integrations or contracted scheduling support would make Looheru a business associate requiring HIPAA contracting and controls.
  • Whether behavioral-health, therapy, disability, and special-needs workflows should be categorically excluded from autonomous voice until a later phase.

Exact consent and disclosure language candidates

Parent in-app authorization for nonclinical voice

I authorize Looheru to contact the businesses and caregivers I select for this task using automated calls, texts, or email to request scheduling information or confirm availability on my behalf. Looheru may identify itself as an automated assistant acting for me. It may not discuss medical, insurance, billing, or other sensitive details without additional authorization.

Parent in-app authorization for healthcare-adjacent scheduling

I authorize Looheru to contact the provider office I select to request or confirm scheduling information only. Looheru may identify itself as an automated assistant acting on my behalf. It may not discuss symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, medications, insurance, billing, or therapy details, and it must stop and hand off to me if the office asks for those details.

Call-start disclosure for MVP no-recording mode

Hello, this is Looheru, an automated scheduling assistant calling on behalf of [Parent first name], with their permission. This is not a sales call. I’m only calling to check availability or confirm a time. If you’d prefer to speak directly with the parent, I can note that and end the call now.

Call-start disclosure if transcription is enabled

Hello, this is Looheru, an automated scheduling assistant calling on behalf of [Parent first name], with their permission. This is not a sales call. This call may be transcribed to capture scheduling details. If you do not want to continue with an automated assistant, please say “human callback” and I will stop.

Healthcare-office variant

Hello, this is Looheru, an automated scheduling assistant calling on behalf of [Parent first name], who authorized this call. I’m not a clinician, and I can only handle scheduling logistics. I’m calling to ask about available appointment times or confirm a specific time. If you need the parent directly, please let me know and I will stop.

Office opt-out language

Understood. I’ll mark this office so automated calls are not used for future requests and will ask the parent to contact you directly.

Open questions and limitations

This report is high-confidence on the major federal distinctions, Utah/California/Colorado AI disclosure issues, HIPAA consumer-vs-provider boundary, and the all-party recording problem. It is not a completed 50-state survey of every state mini-TCPA or autodialer statute, and it does not fully analyze special confidentiality regimes such as substance-use treatment records or every state’s minor-consent rules. The highest-risk unresolved issue for autonomous rollout is the intersection of AI-generated voice + mobile line status + transcription without audio recording. That is the first place I would send to outside counsel for a launch memo.
