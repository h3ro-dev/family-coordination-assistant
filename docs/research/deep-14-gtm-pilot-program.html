June 2026 Operating Plan for

Executive recommendation

Based on the attached packet, the right June 2026 move is a paid, founder-led, tightly scoped 30-day B2C pilot focused only on saved-contact babysitter and caregiver coordination over SMS and email. The goal is not broad launch, employer procurement, or “family autopilot.” The goal is to answer four questions with real behavior, not opinions: Will the right families pay? Will they use it more than once? Can the product complete the task with low enough support labor and COGS? Does the experience create enough relief that users want it again and will refer it?

Current public market norms support that shape. Early-stage startup playbooks still favor founder-led recruiting, manual onboarding, closed-beta structure, and learning from a small set of high-fit users rather than waiting for perfect automation. A current urlY Combinator library noteturn7search19 explicitly argues to charge real money early, a current urlStripe Atlas guideturn24view0 says first customers must be actively recruited one at a time, and a current urlFirst Round beta testing guideturn8view4 recommends curated external testers, documented feedback loops, and a stable-enough production environment before outside use. citeturn7search19turn24view0turn8view4turn8view3

The pricing context also supports a paid but tightly bounded pilot. Family-organizer products like urlOhaiturn1search0, urlGetherturn2search0, urlSenseturn10search0, and urlSkylight Calendar Plusturn1search1 condition consumers to expect free tiers, short free trials, or relatively low monthly pricing, while urlCare.comturn1search2 sells marketplace access at a much lower price than true coordination help. By contrast, task-bundle and concierge-adjacent services such as urlFancy Handsturn15search0, urlPrialtoturn15search1, and urlWellthyturn5search0 cost materially more, because human execution is much more expensive than passive organization software. citeturn16view0turn16view1turn16view2turn16view3turn1search2turn16view5turn16view6turn16view7

Paid pilot thesis and participant design

Exact 30-day paid pilot thesis

Thesis: If 18–24 high-burden households from Tamara’s warm network will pay upfront for a 30-day pilot, complete onboarding, submit at least one saved-contact sitter/caregiver task within their first week, and at least half of activated households reuse it within 30 days while completed-task support minutes stay low enough to preserve healthy contribution margin, then the product has enough evidence to justify product hardening and one sponsored cohort sale.

A stronger version of that same thesis is the one that matters for the proposal:

If families who already have trusted sitters still prefer paying urlLooheruhttps://looheru.ai to do the outreach and follow-up for them, then the product is not merely “another organizer.” It is a paid execution layer.

First task playbook for babysitter and saved-contact coordination

This should be the first-task script in the proposal.

Parent input Parent texts: “Need a sitter Friday 6–10 PM.”

System follow-up if needed Only ask for missing essentials: date, time window, and whether the parent wants the assistant to contact saved sitters now.

Immediate confirmation to parent “Got it. I’m asking your saved sitters now.”

Outreach wave one Send SMS or email to the first relevant set of saved contacts. Keep the message short and parent-authorized.

Example outbound message:

Hi [Name] — I’m helping [Parent Name] coordinate childcare. Are you available on Friday from 6–10 PM? Reply YES, NO, or MAYBE.

Response handling Normalize replies into structured status:

  • YES
  • NO
  • MAYBE / unclear
  • question that requires parent input

Options summary to parent Once you have viable options, text the parent a compact summary and ask for a numeric reply.

Example:

Options found for Friday 6–10 PM: 1. Sarah — yes 2. Jenna — yes after 7 PM Reply 1 or 2

Parent choice Parent replies with number.

Final confirmation Confirm with selected caregiver and send close-the-loop message to parent.

If no one replies Do not let the task silently stall. Move into the takeover logic:

  • same day urgent ask: human review much earlier
  • future ask: second-wave outreach or next-day retry
  • if still no fit, close clearly and ask whether to expand to more saved contacts

The core promise is not “we always find someone.” It is “we take the repetitive chasing work off your plate and bring back the answer cleanly.”

Support model and operating rules

What automation can safely do in June

The attached repo is already strong enough for a real pilot in this narrow wedge: request intake, saved-contact outreach, reply handling, options compilation, parent choice, confirmation, basic commands like STATUS/CANCEL, deduplication, and admin visibility. That means June should lean into the safe workflow the product already supports, instead of stretching into healthcare calls or broad agentic behaviors before the operating proof exists.

Interview scripts

Pre-pilot interview

Use 15 minutes. The purpose is qualification and language capture.

  1. Walk me through the last time you had to line up a sitter or caregiver.
  2. What part took the most energy: remembering who to ask, sending the messages, chasing replies, comparing options, or confirming?
  3. How many people do you usually have to contact before you get to “yes”?
  4. What do you use today: text threads, notes app, spouse, calendar, agency, assistant, something else?
  5. How often does this happen in a typical month?
  6. What would make this feel obviously worth paying for?
  7. What would make you lose trust quickly?
  8. For June, would you rather optimize for lowest cost or highest reliability?
After first completed task

Use within 24 hours of confirmation.

  1. What would you have done manually if the product were not available?
  2. Which step felt most valuable?
  3. Was there any moment you felt confused or uneasy?
  4. Did the assistant reach the right people in the right way?
  5. Was the options summary good enough to act on immediately?
  6. How much time or mental load did this remove for you, from 1 to 5?
  7. At this price, did this feel underpriced, fair, or overpriced? Why?
  8. What would make you use it again next week?
Day-30 interview

Use 20 minutes. This is the sponsor-readiness script.

  1. How many times did you use it, and for what kinds of situations?
  2. Which requests worked best? Which failed or stalled?
  3. Did you trust it more, less, or about the same over the month? Why?
  4. If this disappeared tomorrow, how disappointed would you be?
  5. Would you keep paying for it? Which plan feels right now?
  6. Did it reduce stress, arguments, forgotten follow-ups, or work disruption?
  7. Did you ever hold back from using it? If so, why?
  8. Who else immediately comes to mind that would want this?
  9. What should stay out of scope, even if the product grows?
  10. What is the clearest sentence you would use to describe it to a friend?

Sponsored cohort path

Researched norms versus Looheru-specific recommendation

Researched norms: Public employer and caregiving-benefit pricing is still mostly custom and sales-led, not self-serve. urlCare.comturn17view4 explicitly says pricing varies by team size and selected benefits, while employer pages from urlCleoturn21view0, urlCariloopturn22view1, urlBright Horizonsturn16view10, and urlHelprturn16view13 route buyers into demos or benefits-expert conversations rather than posting simple rate cards. At the high-touch end, urlWellthyturn17view3 publicly lists employer purchase pricing starting at $450 per employee per month, which is a useful signal for what true human care coordination can cost. citeturn17view4turn21view0turn22view1turn16view10turn16view13turn17view3

Budget-anchor norms: Public association and community sponsorship packages frequently live in the low five figures, including a 2026 urlWomen’s Leadership Connection sponsorship pageturn23search0 with a $10,000 top tier and a 2026 urlphysician-conference sponsorship pageturn23search1 listing a $15,000 featured opportunity. These are not caregiving-benefit rate cards, but they are useful budget anchors for what a community or association can plausibly fund without full enterprise procurement. citeturn23search0turn23search1

Looheru-specific recommendation: Sell the first sponsored cohort as a narrow, nonclinical, low-five-figure proof pilot. Do not price it like a full care-concierge program, and do not pretend it is equivalent to broad employer-benefit incumbents.

The key commercial rule is this:

Sell sponsored cohorts as proof-backed, narrow member/workforce relief programs — not as enterprise software rollouts and not as full care concierge replacements.

Open questions and limitations

Public employer-benefit pricing remains opaque. The strongest public norm is that sales-led custom pricing dominates, not that there is one clean PEPM benchmark; urlCare.comturn17view4 says pricing varies by team size and benefits, and most other employers pages emphasize demos or custom conversations instead of posted pricing. citeturn17view4turn21view0turn22view1turn16view10turn16view13

Public “sponsored cohort” price transparency is also limited. The low-five-figure ranges above are therefore recommended budget asks, informed by adjacent community sponsorship pricing and caregiving-benefit buyer behavior, not by a robust database of posted cohort prices. citeturn23search0turn23search1turn17view4turn17view3

The June recommendation also assumes the product stays within the attached packet’s safest wedge: saved-contact coordination over SMS/email only. If scope expands into provider calling, clinical scheduling, or any workflow touching PHI, the support model, legal review, buyer story, and unit economics all change materially.
