Looheru ownership recommendation memo

Executive recommendation

Based on the attached packet, the current founder-facing strategy, the public footprint around entity["people","Tamara Moores Todd","Looheru founder"], the live urlLooheru siteturn13search0, and the still-public family coordination codebase on urlGitHubturn17search3, the right recommendation for the next 90 days is founder-controlled, vendor-first, equity-later-if-earned. In practical terms, that means: a paid 30–60 day Build + Own Sprint with urlUtlyzeturn16search0 and/or urlSolutionStreamturn16search1 under a normal services structure, no upfront co-founder-style equity, no studio-style percentage now, and no transfer of control over the core entity, IP, or production accounts. The public repo is currently visible on GitHub, and the public site appears to be a very light landing page rather than a mature operating stack, which reinforces the need to clean up ownership, access, and production responsibility before deeper partnership economics are introduced. citeturn14view0turn14view1turn0search3turn18view9

This is the founder-protective answer because the market already dilutes founders hard over time. urlCartahttps://carta.com reports that the median founding team retains about 56% after seed and 36% after Series A; urlCRVhttps://www.crv.com repeats essentially the same benchmark. Separate published analyses of venture studios say studio-originated companies often give studios 15% to 50% common ownership, with some studio models sitting in the 25% to 50%+ range and some published descriptions going materially higher. That range may be rational when a studio creates the concept, team, capital, and operating engine from near-zero. It is not a good default for Looheru, where there is already a founder, a concept, a product direction, a repo, and an early public footprint. citeturn18view14turn18view15turn18view7turn18view8turn9search0turn9search1

For the next 90 days, ownership should therefore work like this:

  • Tamara keeps clear founder majority and CEO control.
  • Looheru-specific work product belongs to the company once paid for.
  • The sprint partner keeps only clearly defined background IP and generic reusable know-how.
  • Any later equity must be milestone-based, vesting-based, and contingent on verified operating value, not promised now.

Founder control and governance

The recommended founder-control model for the next 90 days is straightforward: Tamara should remain the sole clear control point economically and operationally. If there is already an entity, she should hold a decisive founder majority on a fully diluted basis; if the entity is not yet properly organized for the intended path, she should form or convert into a structure that preserves control before bringing in any partner economics. For a company that may want venture flexibility, employee equity, warrants, future financing, and cleaner QSBS optionality, the market-standard path is still a C corporation; urlCartahttps://carta.com describes the C-corp as the standard venture-backed structure, while also noting that LLCs fit better when traditional VC is not the likely path. citeturn20view1turn19search5

In governance terms, Tamara should stay CEO and sole or controlling board seat for now. Guidance from urlCooley Gohttps://www.cooleygo.com is useful here: boards delegate day-to-day operations to officers, but material actions still require board approval, including equity grants or transfers, borrowing, amendments to governing documents, hiring or firing senior management, major contracts, and asset sales. For Looheru, that means the founder can and should run the company day to day, but the documents should clearly reserve major structural decisions to founder/board approval rather than allowing any vendor or fractional operator to acquire de facto control through access, custom, or ambiguity. citeturn18view13

The practical CEO decision-rights package should include: final say on product positioning, pricing, pilot design, customer selection, healthcare-adjacent scope expansion, vendor selection and termination, hiring of any executive or contractor with meaningful access, fundraising path, and whether the company remains lean and revenue-first versus goes toward venture financing. The sprint partner can push hard, disagree directly, propose architecture and product changes, and own execution inside an approved scope. They should not own the final call on strategy, budget expansion, external commitments, or any production or legal posture that could bind the company.

The reserved-matters list should be explicit and short enough to use. At minimum, Tamara should not give up unilateral or founder/board approval over: issuing stock, options, or warrants; creating or increasing an option pool; amending the charter, bylaws, or operating agreement; taking debt above an agreed threshold; granting board seats or information rights; selling or exclusively licensing core IP; entering regulated provider, payer, employer, or enterprise agreements; changing payment processor or bank control; changing registrar, DNS, or the assistant number; and launching autonomous healthcare voice without counsel-approved workflows and disclosure. That is both founder-protective and squarely aligned with standard early-stage board practice. citeturn18view13

What Tamara should not give up yet is just as important:

  • board control;
  • majority common ownership;
  • authority to replace the technical partner;
  • ownership of the domain, assistant number, payment account, hosting account, data store, and backups;
  • unilateral approval over fundraising and investor terms;
  • unilateral approval over whether Looheru remains proprietary versus adopts any open-source or shared-IP strategy.

If a later founder-like technical operator is ever brought in on true long-term equity, that equity should vest over time. urlCRVhttps://www.crv.com notes that the standard founder vesting structure is four years with a one-year cliff, and urlCooley Gohttps://www.cooleygo.com notes the related tax and repurchase mechanics, including 83(b) timing for vesting stock. citeturn18view16turn21search2turn21search8

Entity, IP, and account ownership

The cleanest operating rule is: if the asset is specific to Looheru’s business, customers, brand, workflows, or production environment, it should be owned or administratively controlled by the company, not by a vendor or a personal account. That should apply across source control, telephony, hosting, payments, analytics, logs, and backups. Published startup guidance is consistent on why this matters: investors and counterparties care about clean capital structure and clean chain of title, and confusing structures are a due-diligence red flag. citeturn20view1turn18view20

The checklist should look like this:

Legal entity. If Looheru is not already cleanly formed, form a founder-controlled parent now. If there is already an LLC but the likely path includes equity grants, warrants, outside financing, or a studio/fractional operator discussion, do not issue equity first and “clean it later.” Ask counsel whether the entity should remain an LLC for a lean path or be converted into a C-corp before any outside equity is promised. citeturn20view1

Repo and code. The Looheru repo should sit in a company-owned source-control organization, not in a developer’s personal namespace. All secrets, deploy keys, CI tokens, and production branches should be rotated into company control. Because the packet copy of the repo appears to have no open-source license file, counsel should confirm whether any public licensing was intended; absent a license, urlGitHub Docsturn1search0 states that default copyright rules apply and others do not receive rights to reproduce, distribute, or create derivative works. That is not a substitute for chain-of-title cleanup. citeturn18view9

Domain and DNS. The registrar account and DNS should be company-owned with Tamara as primary admin and one backup admin on a company email. No domain should sit only in a contractor’s personal registrar account.

Assistant number and telephony stack. The phone number and the telephony account, including any setup with urlTwilioturn17search0, should be company-owned, billable to the company, and portable. The company should control verified caller IDs, messaging services, webhook settings, and recovery emails. A vendor can be an admin; the vendor should not be the legal or practical owner of the number. citeturn17search0

Email sending and inbound routing. The sending domain, DNS records, admin mailbox, and delivery account, including any setup with urlResendturn17search2, should be company-owned. Vendors can configure automations, but the company should own SPF/DKIM/DMARC settings, shared mailboxes, and inbound-routing endpoints. citeturn17search2

Hosting, database, and backups. If the stack is running on urlRailwayturn16search3 or a similar platform, the company should own the project, billing method, recovery emails, root environment-variable access, database credentials, and backup schedule. Backups should be encrypted, export-tested, and recoverable by the company without the vendor. citeturn16search3

Payments. The payment processor account, including any setup with urlStripeturn17search1, should be in the company name with founder or finance administrative control. That includes payout bank account, tax profile, webhook endpoints, products/prices, and dispute access. A technical partner can implement, but should not own merchant-of-record control. citeturn17search1

Prompts, workflows, and orchestration logic. Everything specific to Looheru’s customer jobs, prompt libraries, task trees, escalation rules, routing logic, outreach templates, evaluation rubrics, and brand voice should be treated as company IP. Generic agent frameworks, generic orchestration components, and reusable internal tooling can remain partner background IP if they are scheduled and defined in writing.

Customer, pilot, and product data. Customer lists, pilot usage data, transcripts, structured outputs, support notes, analytics events, and safety logs should belong to the company, with the partner acting as service provider or processor under contract. The company should have export rights, deletion rights, retention controls, and a clear offboarding path. That matters even more if the product ever drifts closer to healthcare-adjacent metadata. citeturn18view20

Brand, copy, design, and analytics. The wordmark, landing-page copy, onboarding copy, email/SMS templates, product designs, and analytics dashboards should all be assigned to the company. Analytics and logging workspaces should be company-owned, with vendor access removable on notice.

On contributor cleanup, the market guidance is also clear. urlCooley Gohttps://www.cooleygo.com explains that invention-assignment agreements are a standard feature for both employees and contractors, and that consultant agreements should expressly state that confidential information stays protected and that work and inventions arising from the engagement belong to the company, not the contractor. urlCartahttps://carta.com also points founders to written advisor agreements that define scope, vesting, and IP/confidentiality. citeturn18view10turn18view11turn18view12turn22view0

That leads to these contributor recommendations:

  • Prior contributors: inventory everyone who touched code, prompts, copy, design, infra, strategy decks, or customer-facing materials. Get signed assignment documents now. If anyone will not sign, quarantine and rewrite their contributions.
  • Contractors: every contractor should sign a consulting agreement with confidentiality, present-tense IP assignment, background-IP schedule, open-source disclosure obligation, and a ban on undeclared subcontracting.
  • Advisors: use a written advisor agreement; if the advisor creates material work product, use consulting-style IP language, not a casual advisory letter.
  • Utlyze/SolutionStream: use an MSA + SOW for the sprint. Company-specific deliverables are assigned to Looheru upon payment. The partner retains scheduled background IP and generic know-how, but grants Looheru a broad, perpetual, transferable license to any embedded partner background IP required to use the deliverables without interruption.
  • Background IP versus venture-specific IP: do not use joint ownership. It is usually a mess. Use a scheduled distinction instead: generalized tools stay with the partner; anything specific to Looheru’s business, customers, workflows, or production system belongs to Looheru.

Relationship structures and economics

The decision-rights model should change with the relationship type, but founder control should not disappear.

In a paid sprint/vendor model, Tamara owns the “what” and acceptance. The partner owns the “how” inside the approved scope. Founder approval should be required for production deployments, scope expansion, external commitments, account changes, and any change that materially affects compliance, user promises, or budget.

In a fractional CTO/product partner model, the operator can own architecture recommendations, engineering cadence, technical hiring recommendations, vendor oversight, and incident coordination inside an approved mandate. Tamara still owns company strategy, pricing, fundraising, customer commitments, and all reserved matters. This role earns more trust and some authority, but not independent company control.

In a venture studio build partner model, if Looheru ever truly wants one, governance should be explicit in documents from day one. Even then, founder-protective structure is still possible: Tamara as CEO, no immediate equal-board arrangement, and milestone-based equity tranches rather than an upfront studio block. For Looheru specifically, a studio should be treated as something to consider after the sprint, not before it.

In an advisory-only model, the advisor has no operating authority, no production-admin authority, no hiring/firing authority, and no unilateral roadmap ownership. This is advice, not management.

The market anchors behind that table are important to state plainly. Pre-seed advisor grants are usually modest: urlCartahttps://carta.com reports median advisor equity around 0.21% to 0.25%, with a typical range around 0.1% to 1.0%, and the urlFounder Institutehttps://fi.co FAST framework says startups sometimes think of a broader advisor pool in the aggregate rather than handing out large individual grants. Fractional CTO pricing is much less standardized, but published current guides cluster roughly around $150–$300/hour and roughly $8,000–$25,000/month depending on scope and seniority. Studio economics, by contrast, are much larger when studios truly originate the company: published descriptions cite 15% to 50% common stock as typical for studio-opco founder stakes, with many studio analyses placing operational ownership in the 25% to 50% range and some descriptions higher. Warrants are more commonly used for financing or strategic/commercial transactions than as routine build compensation. citeturn18view0turn18view1turn18view2turn18view3turn18view4turn18view5turn18view7turn18view8turn9search0turn18view19turn18view18

For this specific Looheru situation, the suggested ranges should be:

  • Advisor equity: normally 0.10%–0.25%; exceptional, tightly scoped advisor 0.25%–0.50%; almost never 1%.
  • Fractional CTO equity: generally 0.25%–1.00% if paired with real cash compensation; stretch to 1.25%–1.50% only if the role is genuinely embedded, multi-quarter, and accountable for shipping and team/process ownership.
  • Venture studio equity: 0% now; later consider 5%–12% earned in tranches if there is real added team, sustained operating responsibility, and measurable pilot traction; only move toward 12%–15% if the studio is effectively adding co-founder-level execution plus meaningful capital or paid-in-kind team resources.
  • Warrants: usually 0.25%–1.00% max, only as a narrow substitute for some cash or as part of a specific strategic arrangement; exercise terms, trigger events, and expiry should be explicit.
  • Revenue share: 2%–8% of net pilot revenue, capped at roughly 1.25x–2.0x of the foregone fee, sunset after 12–18 months or upon change of control / priced financing.

The reason equity-only build should not be offered now is simple: the facts that justify equity are not fully known yet. The legal entity may still need confirmation, the contributor IP chain may still need cleanup, the public repo suggests the code posture is still exposed, the pilot economics are not yet proven, and the working relationship under stress has not yet been earned. On top of that, messy capital structures are a known investor red flag. citeturn18view20turn14view1turn18view9

Equity gates, exits, and red flags

Before any equity is granted to anyone other than the founder, all of the following should be true:

  • the legal entity and signing authority are confirmed;
  • the cap table is accurate and current;
  • prior contributors are identified and their assignments are signed, or the affected work has been rewritten;
  • all production-critical accounts are company-controlled;
  • the role, time commitment, and decision rights are written down;
  • the equity instrument is selected by counsel and board-approved;
  • any required 409A, valuation, or tax work is handled;
  • vesting or milestone mechanics are explicit;
  • sprint deliverables have been accepted;
  • there is real evidence that the relationship should continue beyond vendor work;
  • the company knows what it is actually paying for: build capacity, operating accountability, strategic leverage, or all three.

If founder or founder-like stock is being issued subject to vesting, the tax mechanics also matter. urlCooley Gohttps://www.cooleygo.com notes that 83(b) elections must be filed within 30 days to be effective, and both urlCartahttps://carta.com and other tax guidance emphasize that C-corp structure can matter for QSBS planning. That is one more reason not to improvise equity grants casually. citeturn21search2turn21search8turn21search1turn21search13

The clean exit mechanics should also be proposal-ready now, before anyone gets attached to a role. The sprint agreement should include: termination for convenience on short written notice; payment for work performed through termination; immediate assignment of all paid-for deliverables; a mandatory transition-assistance window; handoff of repo, credentials, docs, architecture diagrams, infra map, vendor list, and backup/export procedures; and survival of the company’s license to any embedded background IP needed to keep operating. Unvested equity or unearned tranches should stop immediately on termination. Vested options should follow the agreed post-termination exercise window. There should be no “hostage” dependency where the founder cannot operate the business without the exiting partner. Standard startup governance sources are clear that equity transfers, warrants, and similar instruments should be board-approved and documented, not handled informally. citeturn18view13turn18view18turn18view19

The red flags that should block production access or operating responsibility are these:

  • unknown or disputed code ownership;
  • any prior contributor who expects equity, compensation, or ongoing rights without signed documents;
  • any production account living solely in a contractor’s or vendor’s personal tenant;
  • inability to export data, backups, logs, or payment history;
  • lack of founder admin access to registrar, telephony, payments, or hosting;
  • no signed consulting agreement with IP assignment and confidentiality;
  • public repos that contain proprietary production logic, secrets, or customer data without a deliberate open-source strategy;
  • healthcare-adjacent voice calling without counsel review, disclosures, human handoff rules, and recording-law analysis;
  • founder use of employer time, systems, confidential information, or possibly overlapping employer IP;
  • any partner who asks for joint ownership of the core product, perpetual revenue rights, or broad vetoes before pilot proof;
  • any request for equity before the role, milestones, and offboarding mechanics are explicit.

The employer-conflict point deserves special attention. urlCooley Gohttps://www.cooleygo.com specifically notes that “moonlighting” can be thorny for founders. If Tamara is still employed by urlIntermountain Healthturn16search2 or another institution while building Looheru, counsel should review all employment, confidentiality, moonlighting, invention-assignment, and public-title restrictions before the company takes on production risk or outside equity participants. citeturn18view17

Proposal language and counsel pack

A direct founder-protective paragraph you can use in the proposal is:

Our recommendation is to keep Looheru founder-controlled and start with a paid Build + Own Sprint, not a co-founder or studio equity structure. In this first phase, Tamara keeps majority ownership, CEO decision rights, and control of all core accounts, data, and company-specific IP. We will move fast, disagree directly, and help harden the product into a pilot-ready business, but we do not think she should give up board control, account control, or meaningful equity before the IP chain is clean, the pilot is real, and the working relationship has earned a deeper structure.

A second paragraph, if you want the “not lawyerly” commercial framing, is:

If the sprint proves out, we can revisit the relationship in a more durable form. At that point, the right options are a scoped build continuation, a fractional CTO/product partner role with clear deliverables and vesting, or a carefully structured studio partnership with earned tranches. What we should not do is price a long-term ownership relationship before we have evidence, clean ownership records, and a trusted operating rhythm.

The counsel checklist should be concrete. Bring these documents and questions to a startup attorney:

Bring these documents

  • formation documents, bylaws or operating agreement, EIN records, stock or unit issuances;
  • current cap table;
  • any SAFE, note, warrant, or side-letter documents;
  • founder stock purchase agreements and vesting documents;
  • proof of any 83(b) filings;
  • any stock plan or option plan drafts;
  • contributor list for code, prompts, design, copy, decks, and infra;
  • all contractor, consultant, and advisor agreements;
  • any standalone IP assignment documents;
  • registrar and DNS records;
  • telephony, hosting, source-control, email, analytics, and payment account ownership records;
  • privacy policy, terms, pilot consent language, and any draft DPA;
  • employment agreement and any IP/confidentiality/moonlighting documents from the founder’s current employer;
  • draft MSA/SOW for the sprint partner.

Ask these exact questions

  • Is the current entity the right one, or should Looheru form/convert before any outside equity?
  • Is the company’s chain of title clean for code, prompts, copy, design, and data assets?
  • Which prior contributions need ratification, assignment, or rewrite?
  • What is the right instrument for any later partner upside: options, restricted stock, warrants, or something else?
  • What board and stockholder approvals are required before any equity, warrant, or major services agreement is issued?
  • How should background IP versus venture-specific IP be defined in the sprint agreement?
  • What transition and license rights are needed so Looheru can continue operating if the partner leaves?
  • Are there employer-side IP, conflict, or moonlighting issues that could taint ownership?
  • What TCPA, call-recording, privacy, consumer-health-data, HIPAA, or other legal boundaries apply to SMS, email, and future voice workflows?
  • What should the company absolutely refuse to collect, store, or automate in the first pilot?
  • Should the company file trademark applications now, or wait until after pilot naming is settled?
  • What 409A, QSBS, and tax steps should be taken before any equity grants or founder-vesting changes?
