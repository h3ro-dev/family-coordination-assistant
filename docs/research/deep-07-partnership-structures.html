Partnership structures for Looheru

Situation read

Using the uploaded packet as the primary private context, the core strategic read is: this is not a blank-sheet startup, but it is also not ready for an open-ended “we’ll just help however needed” partnership. Public evidence supports the packet’s view that entity["people","Tamara Moores Todd","Looheru founder and Intermountain physician informatics leader"] brings unusually strong founder-market fit: urlIntermountain Healthhttps://intermountainhealthcare.org named her chief health informatics officer in May 2025, while the public urlLooheru.aihttps://looheru.ai website is still only an under-construction page. That combination usually means the value of the next engagement is not branding or deck polish; it is clarifying ownership, hardening the product, and choosing the right operating relationship before expectations sprawl. citeturn18view0turn15view0

The public urlfamily-coordination-assistant repositoryhttps://github.com/h3ro-dev/family-coordination-assistant already contains a real Phase 1 baseline: an SMS/email coordination loop, a deterministic voice path, an admin UI, durable workflow state, and tests. But that same public README says the project still needs production hardening, including webhook signature verification, abuse protection, stronger auth than a single admin token, and a clear non-PHI boundary or HIPAA-grade controls if healthcare use cases expand. That is exactly why the packet’s instinct to avoid jumping directly into a vague venture-studio promise is sound. citeturn15view1turn19view2

The public positioning of urlSolutionStreamturn1search1 aligns with the packet’s stated capabilities: software delivery, project development, staffing, and fractional leadership. urlUtlyzeturn2search0 publicly describes itself as an AI-native venture studio, and the adjacent leverage mentioned in the packet also has public support in urlNew Rewardturn2search1 and urlVuplicityturn1search3. In other words, the technical partner credibly can bring execution capacity, but the structure has to preserve the founder’s likely preference for majority control, speed, direct disagreement, and a slim model. citeturn21view0turn2search4turn2search1turn1search3

General market norms

General market norms are helpful here, but they should be treated as guardrails rather than templates. Light advisor compensation is usually modest. The urlFounder Institute FAST Agreementturn3search0 is explicitly designed for strategic advisors rather than project consulting or work-for-hire relationships. urlCarta’s advisory shares guideturn3search1 reports a median pre-seed advisor grant of 0.21% in H1 2024, and its broader advisor guide says a typical range is about 0.1% to 1%, with advisor vesting often structured over two years with monthly vesting and a shorter horizon than employees. citeturn17view0turn9view1turn9view2turn11view3

True venture-studio economics are materially different. urlHigh Alpha’s venture studio explainerturn3search6 describes studios as co-creating with founders and partners, then supporting the company financially and through hands-on operational services. urlIdealabturn3search3 describes a model of testing ideas, recruiting teams, spinning them into companies, and helping them grow. Public studio guides vary widely on equity, with some describing roughly 15% to 40% and others 30% to 60% or more, which is less a clean benchmark than proof that studio economics are negotiated case by case and are much closer to cofounder economics than advisor economics. citeturn9view3turn9view5turn12search7turn7search1

Instrument choice matters. urlCooley GO’s warrants guideturn4search1 notes that stock options are commonly used for employees and service providers, while warrants are typically used to sweeten a financial or commercial transaction with an investor, lender, or strategic partner. That makes warrants potentially useful in a strategic build-partner arrangement, but a poor default substitute for ordinary consulting compensation. citeturn9view7

Formal papering is non-negotiable. urlCarta’s advisor-agreement guidanceturn3search13 says advisor agreements should spell out role, time commitment, shares or options, vesting, confidentiality, and IP clauses. urlCooley’s IP assignment guidanceturn5search0 and its contractor-agreement checklist say contractor-created work is not owned by the company without an explicit present assignment. The urlU.S. Copyright Office’s Circular 30 on works made for hireturn5search2 also makes clear that “work made for hire” is narrower than many startup operators assume and requires specific facts and, for commissioned works, a signed written agreement. citeturn11view0turn9view8turn9view9turn10view0turn10view1turn10view2

Design principles for this situation

For this specific situation, the right design principles are straightforward. The structure should preserve Tamara’s majority ownership and CEO authority. It should make the technical partner highly accountable for engineering, but only inside a defined lane. It should stay slim enough for a speed-oriented founder. And it should force uncomfortable issues into the open early: who owns what, who controls which accounts, who can deploy, who carries operating responsibility, and what data is allowed in the system. Because the public baseline already exists, the next question is not whether something can be built. It is whether the current product, ownership chain, and pilot plan are clean enough to justify deeper entanglement. The repo’s own hardening list reinforces that sequencing. citeturn15view1

A second design principle is to keep the first operating boundary narrow and nonclinical. The repo already supports family coordination and a voice-adjacent pathway, but the compliance bar rises quickly if PHI or healthcare coordination enters the loop. Public guidance from urlTwiliohttps://www.twilio.com and urlRailwayhttps://railway.com frames HIPAA as a shared-responsibility model, not something a vendor “solves” by itself, and Twilio’s public HIPAA material says customers need both a BAA and HIPAA-eligible products. Meanwhile, the public security page for urlResendhttps://resend.com emphasizes encryption, backups, GDPR, and SOC 2, but the page reviewed here did not itself establish a HIPAA/BAA path. That supports a strong recommendation: keep the first pilot strictly non-PHI unless vendor eligibility and contracts are verified directly in writing. citeturn19view1turn19view0turn19view3turn20view0turn13view0

Below, “the technical partner” means urlUtlyzeturn2search0 and/or urlSolutionStreamturn1search1.

Partnership options

Paid discovery and solution map only

When it fits: This fits when code ownership, account ownership, contributor expectations, or the actual production state are still unclear enough that nobody should touch production yet.

Scope: A fast, fixed-fee discovery focused on current-state reality, ownership/account mapping, pilot scope, architecture boundaries, operating model, and estimate bands for later phases.

Deliverables: A decision memo, current-state map, ownership-and-access checklist, scoped pilot definition, risk register, recommended sequencing, and commercial options for the next step.

Duration: Five to ten business days.

Cash economics: $10,000 to $18,000 fixed fee, ideally split 50% up front and 50% at delivery. A credit-forward structure is cleaner than using equity here.

Possible equity, revenue-share, or warrant economics: None recommended. If Tamara is cash-sensitive, credit 50% to 100% of the discovery fee into the next signed phase within 30 days rather than improvising equity for consulting work.

Decision rights: Tamara retains all decisions. The technical partner only frames options and tradeoffs.

IP ownership: On full payment, Tamara or the company owns the final deliverables; the technical partner retains background know-how, reusable templates, and generic methods.

Responsibilities for Tamara: Provide access to whatever can legally and practically be reviewed, answer ownership and contributor questions directly, and pick the decision criteria for the next phase.

Responsibilities for the technical partner: Produce decision-grade specificity, not a strategy deck that simply restates the problem.

Risks: Analysis without risk reduction. A fast-moving founder may experience this as delay if it is not tightly run.

Clean exit or transition path: Deliver the solution map, hold a readout, and either stop cleanly or roll directly into option two or three.

What must be true before selecting it: There is not yet enough trust, access, or legal clarity to let anyone accept engineering responsibility.

Technical audit and hardening sprint

When it fits: This is the best fit when there is already a working baseline and the immediate need is to make it safer, more reliable, and pilot-ready.

Scope: Review the active code and deployment posture against the public baseline: workflow reliability, auth, secrets and access handling, webhook security, logging, retention, backup assumptions, pilot instrumentation, and the explicit no-PHI boundary.

Deliverables: A severity-ranked audit, hardening backlog, patched code or pull requests for agreed issues, pilot launch-readiness checklist, basic incident/runbook documentation, and KPI instrumentation requirements.

Duration: One week for audit, plus one to three additional weeks for hardening, depending on scope.

Cash economics: Roughly $12,000 to $18,000 for the audit, plus $18,000 to $40,000 for the hardening sprint.

Possible equity, revenue-share, or warrant economics: None is the clean default. If the fee is meaningfully discounted because both parties expect a broader strategic relationship, a very small warrant sidecar of roughly 0.25% to 0.75% can be defended more easily than ad hoc “advisory equity,” because a warrant is more naturally tied to a commercial partnership than to ordinary services. citeturn9view7

Decision rights: Tamara approves business tradeoffs, scope, and pilot boundary. The technical partner should control how the fixes are implemented, provided they stay inside agreed scope and budget.

IP ownership: Tamara or the company owns all venture-specific fixes, code, and runbooks on payment. Reusable modules and pre-existing playbooks stay with the technical partner.

Responsibilities for Tamara: Grant code, hosting, messaging, and email access; answer who owns which accounts; decide whether the pilot is strictly nonclinical; and be available for fast decisions.

Responsibilities for the technical partner: Surface real risks, fix the agreed critical issues, and avoid quietly expanding scope into a de facto rebuild.

Risks: The audit may reveal that parts of the current stack should be rebuilt or reassigned. It may also surface ownership issues nobody wants to confront.

Clean exit or transition path: A clean handoff package, walkthrough, credential rotation plan, and optional follow-on statement of work for build or fractional CTO support.

What must be true before selecting it: Tamara can grant meaningful technical access, and the near-term goal is a 30-60 day pilot rather than a months-long rebuild.

Scoped build project

When it fits: This fits when the pilot scope is clear enough to contract against, and Tamara wants a vendor relationship rather than an embedded executive or studio partner.

Scope: Build or extend a defined set of workflows, admin/support tooling, instrumentation, and launch support against explicit acceptance criteria.

Deliverables: Working product increments, tests, deployment support, documentation, and a short warranty/stabilization period after launch.

Duration: Usually six to ten weeks for a tight pilot scope.

Cash economics: Roughly $60,000 to $140,000, milestone-based, depending on whether the work mostly hardens/extents the current baseline or introduces larger new surfaces.

Possible equity, revenue-share, or warrant economics: Default to cash-only. If the founder wants to reduce cash outlay, use one financing lever, not three: either a small warrant sidecar, or a short-term capped revenue share that sunsets after a defined repayment multiple, or a modest fee deferral. Avoid stacking a big discount, equity, and perpetual revenue share together.

Decision rights: Tamara should own product acceptance, priorities, and customer-facing choices. The technical partner should own implementation details, internal engineering process, and staffing of the contracted work.

IP ownership: Tamara or the company owns the deliverables once paid for; the technical partner keeps pre-existing tooling and generic components.

Responsibilities for Tamara: Act as a single empowered decision-maker, approve scope weekly, provide content and pilot-user context, and accept or reject milestones promptly.

Responsibilities for the technical partner: Deliver against the contract, avoid scope drift, and keep the founder informed about tradeoffs rather than silently compensating for ambiguity.

Risks: This can devolve into change-order conflict if the first pilot is still too fuzzy. It can also create a “build and disappear” dynamic that leaves nobody truly owning the ongoing product.

Clean exit or transition path: Thirty-day warranty, then either maintenance, a handoff to Tamara’s team, or a move into option four.

What must be true before selecting it: The next workload is specific enough to describe in acceptance criteria, and Tamara does not want or need a delegated technical executive.

Fractional CTO and product partner

When it fits: This is the best fit when Tamara wants one accountable technical operator without giving up company control or jumping into a full studio structure.

Scope: Architecture, release management, contractor/vendor management, pilot instrumentation, roadmap stewardship, security posture, and ongoing product/technical decision support.

Deliverables: A rolling 90-day roadmap, weekly operating cadence, release notes, KPI reviews, hiring/vendor scorecards, and technical decision memos.

Duration: Minimum three months; six months is more realistic if the role is expected to carry pilot accountability.

Cash economics: Roughly $8,000 to $18,000 per month, depending on whether this is one day per week of true oversight or a more embedded two-day-per-week role with team management.

Possible equity, revenue-share, or warrant economics: A reasonable range is 0.5% to 2.0%, vested monthly over 24 months with a three-month cliff or tied partly to milestones. That is intentionally above lightweight advisor economics and intentionally below a true studio stake. It reflects that this role carries real operating accountability, not occasional advice. Published advisor norms are much smaller than studio economics, which is why this middle path exists. citeturn9view2turn11view3turn9view3turn12search7

Decision rights: Tamara remains CEO and retains final say on company strategy, pricing, GTM, fundraising, and hiring of senior business roles. The fractional CTO should receive delegated authority for architecture, release cadence, engineering priorities inside budget, and vendor recommendations.

IP ownership: All venture-specific work product should belong to the company, with invention assignment from every subcontractor or contributor.

Responsibilities for Tamara: Make decisions quickly, fund the role meaningfully, and allow the delegated technical leader to actually own the technical lane.

Responsibilities for the technical partner: Be accountable for outcomes, not just recommendations, and keep the founder out of unnecessary engineering detail.

Risks: The common failure mode is ambiguity: a costly advisor with no authority, or a de facto executive without executive protections.

Clean exit or transition path: Thirty-day notice, documented handoff, vendor/account transfer map, and either replacement by a full-time hire or rollback to advisory support.

What must be true before selecting it: Tamara genuinely wants to delegate technical leadership, there is budget for it, and one person is empowered to make founder decisions without committee drag.

Venture studio build partner

When it fits: This fits only if Tamara wants a genuine co-building partner, not just a vendor, and only after ownership, contributor expectations, and the initial pilot thesis are clarified.

Scope: Product building, technical execution, operating model design, analytics, pilot execution support, GTM experimentation, and possibly fundraising prep and staffing.

Deliverables: Pilot-ready product, operating dashboard, documented workflows, hire plan, investor/demo artifacts, and a stage-gated company-building plan.

Duration: Initial six- to twelve-month commitment with explicit stage gates.

Cash economics: A practical range would be $10,000 to $30,000 per month in a reduced-cash structure, or $25,000 to $60,000 per month in a fuller-cash structure with more embedded capacity.

Possible equity, revenue-share, or warrant economics: This is where discipline matters most. Public venture-studio material describes deeper hands-on support, capital, and materially larger ownership than advisory work, and public operator guides show wide ranges that can easily swamp founder control if imported lazily. In this specific case, because there is already a founder, concept, and baseline product, a reasonable studio-style recommendation would be lower and more conditional than generic “studio” talk: perhaps 10% to 20% earned in tranches against clearly defined deliverables, capital, or time commitments, and in some cases even 5% to 12% if cash stays meaningful and no capital is invested. Do not stack large equity, perpetual revenue share, and large cash fees together. citeturn9view3turn9view5turn12search7turn7search1

Decision rights: Tamara should remain CEO and majority owner. If the technical partner earns a studio stake, any reserved matters should be very specific: core-architecture changes that force a rebuild, senior technical hiring, financing terms tied to the partner’s equity, sale or license of IP, or major budget overruns. A board observer seat is easier to justify early than an open-ended veto.

IP ownership: All venture-specific IP should sit in the company, not the studio. Every contributor should assign into the company, with background IP and reusable tools carved out narrowly.

Responsibilities for Tamara: Lead customer learning, brand, GTM, fundraising, and high-conviction product calls; show up at founder intensity.

Responsibilities for the technical partner: Commit real capacity and operating attention, not just a brand label or a promise of future help.

Risks: Founder-control tension, future financing friction, mispriced dilution before proof, and emotional resentment if the relationship feels like “services wearing equity clothing.”

Clean exit or transition path: Equity should vest or earn in tranches; there should be a clear buy-back or repurchase framework, termination mechanics, support tail, and deadlock process.

What must be true before selecting it: Clean chain of title, a formed company, explicit cap-table authority, aligned founder expectations, budget or financing plan, and enough evidence that a deeper partner will increase speed more than it increases drag.

Advisory-only relationship

When it fits: This fits only if Tamara already has builders and mainly wants judgment, pattern recognition, introductions, and occasional architecture/product review.

Scope: Biweekly or monthly calls, async review, selected introductions, and memo-level decision support.

Deliverables: Review notes, decision memos, intro support, light diligence or roadmap feedback.

Duration: Two to six months.

Cash economics: Roughly $2,000 to $5,000 per month, or a fixed block of advisory time.

Possible equity, revenue-share, or warrant economics: If compensated in equity, keep it inside true advisor norms: roughly 0.1% to 0.35% over 12 to 24 months, with monthly vesting and a short cliff. If the founder expects hands-on shipping, this should not be the structure. citeturn9view2turn11view3turn17view0

Decision rights: None. Advice only.

IP ownership: No code or operational work should be implied. If any work product is created, assign it explicitly.

Responsibilities for Tamara: Execute independently and avoid converting advisory calls into unpaid product management or engineering.

Responsibilities for the technical partner: Stay in advisory boundaries and avoid shadow-operating the business without authority.

Risks: This is the easiest structure to misunderstand. It often degenerates into disappointed expectations on both sides.

Clean exit or transition path: Thirty-day cancellation, no operational dependency.

What must be true before selecting it: Tamara already has execution horsepower elsewhere and genuinely wants advice, not delivery.

Recommended path

Safest first step: Option two, structured as a fixed-fee technical audit plus hardening sprint, with a short solution-map component in the first week. That recommendation is driven by the public repo itself: a real baseline exists, and the published gap list is exactly the kind of work an early partner should address before taking on broader build or operating responsibility. It is safer and more useful than a pure discovery deck, and far cleaner than pretending a studio relationship exists before the foundation is verified. citeturn15view1

Highest-upside step: Option five has the highest upside in the abstract, because it can align founder, product, and technical capacity over a longer interval. But it is not the highest-upside next signature. The highest-upside responsible path from today is option two, then option four if the working rhythm is strong, and only then option five if pilot evidence and governance clarity justify it.

What should not be offered yet: Do not offer an equity-only build. Do not offer an undefined “technical partner” title that quietly includes production responsibility, on-call expectations, or healthcare-adjacent liability. Do not offer a broad healthcare or PHI-bearing operating posture just because the founder has healthcare credibility. And do not offer a venture-studio cap-table bite before the February repo provenance, later code provenance, account ownership, and contributor expectations are all explicit in writing. The public repo’s own hardening list and the legal/IP guidance above make that restraint the professional answer, not a timid one. citeturn15view1turn9view8turn9view9turn10view0

Negotiation questions for Tamara:

  • What does she want to remain solely hers in decision rights, and what does she actually want to delegate?
  • Who owns the current code, the domain, the phone number, the hosting project, the messaging accounts, and any test-family data?
  • Is the current live implementation the February public baseline, a fork of it, or a separate codebase?
  • What role, if any, do prior contributors expect in credit, compensation, ownership, or future participation?
  • What is the first pilot promise she would be proud to sell to 10 to 25 families by June or July 2026?
  • What monthly budget can she commit to for the next 90 days without assuming external financing?
  • Does she want a vendor, a delegated technical executive, or an actual co-building partner?
  • What would feel unfair to her in a partnership, even if it looked efficient on paper?
  • If the pilot works, does she want to raise, stay small and profitable, or keep the company intentionally lean?
  • What is off-limits for version one: healthcare scheduling, PHI, autonomous outbound voice, employer branding, or minors’ sensitive data?

Terms that must be explicit before anyone touches production or accepts operating responsibility

The first set of terms is legal ownership. The company name, signatory, cap-table authority, contributor history, and present-tense IP assignment from every person who has created code, product artifacts, prompts, copy, or data structures should be explicit. Contractor language should include confidentiality plus present assignment of inventions and work product, because absent that, ownership assumptions are fragile. “Work made for hire” wording alone should not be relied on casually. citeturn9view8turn9view9turn10view0turn10view1turn10view2

The second set of terms is account and environment control. The contract should say who owns the repository, hosting workspace, phone number, email-sending account, DNS, analytics, secrets manager, and backups; who has admin access; how MFA is handled; who can rotate credentials; and what happens on termination. The public repo’s own published hardening list makes this especially important because it still describes a pilot-grade admin model and additional work needed for production security. citeturn15view1

The third set of terms is data boundary and compliance posture. Before operating responsibility is accepted, the parties should explicitly state whether the pilot is non-PHI only, what data classes are prohibited, how long logs are retained, who can see transcripts and message history, and what happens if an end user sends sensitive health information anyway. If PHI ever enters scope, the partner agreement should require vendor-level validation and executed BAAs where needed, not assumptions. citeturn19view1turn19view0turn19view3turn13view0

The fourth set of terms is security and incident handling. The repo should not be treated as production-ready until webhook validation, auth, abuse protection, logging, backup assumptions, and release authority are explicit. The messaging provider’s own webhook documentation says requests should be signature-validated and handled over HTTPS, and government cloud-shared-responsibility guidance emphasizes incident playbooks, IAM, data security, and responsibility clarity between customer and provider. citeturn19view2turn13view0

The fifth set of terms is operational accountability. Who is on call. Who approves deploys. Who can roll back. What uptime or response expectations exist, if any. Whether there is a warranty window or an SLA. Whether the technical partner is only shipping code or also accepting incident management, support escalation, and vendor coordination.

The sixth set of terms is economics and exit. Payment milestones, acceptance criteria, change-order mechanics, and any equity instrument should be explicit before work starts. If equity exists, the contract should state instrument type, vesting, cliff, milestone triggers, board approval, tax responsibility, termination treatment, repurchase or buy-back rights, and whether acceleration exists. If a warrant or revenue share is used, it should be capped, time-bounded, and not layered on top of multiple other upside instruments without a specific reason.

Open questions and limitations

This report uses the uploaded packet as the primary private context plus public web research, but it does not include direct inspection of the current private production deployment, current private repo, legal entity documents, cap table, or any signed contributor agreements. It also could not independently verify whether the current live product is the same as the February public baseline or a separate implementation. Finally, if the roadmap expands into automated healthcare calling, minors’ sensitive data, or PHI-bearing workflows, state-law voice rules and vendor contract eligibility would need direct legal and contractual verification before any partner should accept operating responsibility.
