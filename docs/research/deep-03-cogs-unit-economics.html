Looheru First-Pass COGS and Unit Economics

I treated the uploaded bundle as the baseline operating model: an SMS-first coordination orchestrator, not a marketplace, with saved-contact outreach, durable state in Postgres, worker retries, retention cleanup, admin tooling, and voice-path scaffolding. On that basis, the economic answer is fairly clear. If Looheru launches with only the baseline babysitter workflows, the core telecom and email costs are low enough that a consumer subscription can work. The real margin risk is not SMS or email. It is workflow creep into browser work, provider calling, and especially human escalation. In practical terms: $29/month is viable for a tightly-scoped launch focused on workflows like babysitter coordination; $20/month can work only with strict fair-use and no included high-cost tasks; $40/month becomes the minimum comfortable price once you include complex browser/email execution; and healthcare/provider voice or concierge work should be either a higher tier or metered credits, not “unlimited” usage under a low fixed monthly fee.

My first-pass model uses these working assumptions. For U.S. toll-free SMS, I assume roughly 1.23¢ outbound and 0.98¢ inbound per single-segment SMS after adding a blended carrier-fee allowance to Twilio’s base SMS rates. For outbound voice, I assume 1.4¢ per minute. For the current scripted Twilio voice flow, I assume 1.8¢ per <Gather> speech-recognition use. For email, I assume an effective marginal cost of about 0.1¢ per message because both Resend and Postmark are effectively sub-cent at startup volume. For hosting, I assume a small two-process API/worker deployment plus Postgres on Railway, allocating about $0.04–$0.10 per task depending on complexity. For payment processing, I use Stripe’s standard 2.9% + 30¢ online-card fee per monthly subscription charge. For labor, I assume $32/hour loaded for routine ops/support and $40/hour loaded for true concierge escalation. citeturn0search1turn1search0turn2search1turn3search0turn8search0turn9search0turn10search1turn10search3

Two implementation details matter more than they look. First, Twilio bills SMS per segment, and non-GSM characters cut the single-segment limit from 160 characters to 70 characters, so message-template hygiene can move cost more than raw send counts suggest. Second, Twilio’s own message resource exposes numSegments as non-PII while marking message body as PII, which makes it possible to instrument telecom cost precisely without storing unnecessary raw content. citeturn18search0turn18search1turn18search2

The voice case deserves one extra note. Margin by subscription price

To make the monthly prices comparable, I stress-tested each price point against four completed tasks per month of a single workflow. That is roughly weekly usage and is a useful “does this subscription break under real engagement?” test. The pricing conclusion is straightforward. A low-price subscription absolutely can support workflows one and two. It cannot safely support workflows three through five if usage is even moderately frequent. Under this stress test, $20 and $29 are economically healthy for baseline sitter coordination, but they are underwater or near-zero for browser-heavy and provider-voice work. $40 becomes the first price point that can comfortably carry some complex execution. $79 is the first price point that can responsibly include limited voice-provider coordination. Even $99 should not imply “unlimited concierge”; it is better framed as a premium subscription with a small included concierge allowance. citeturn9search0

A second way to look at the same issue is breakeven completed tasks per subscriber per month. At $20 net of fees, the model supports roughly 25+ baseline sitter tasks, about 3 browser-heavy tasks, about 3 provider-voice tasks, or only about 1 concierge task before contribution margin goes to zero. At $40, it supports roughly 50+ baseline sitter tasks, about 6 browser-heavy tasks, about 6 provider-voice tasks, or only about 2–3 concierge tasks. That is why the pricing conversation should be framed around task mix, not just subscriber count.

Fair use, credits, and minimum viable price

The cleanest structure is not unlimited usage. It is a subscription plus task credits tied to workflow intensity. I would define a core coordination credit as one completed sitter-style task using saved contacts, up to four outreach contacts, one retry cycle, and no human takeover. On that basis, workflows one and two should cost 1 credit each, browser/email activity tasks should cost 3 credits, provider voice tasks should cost 4 credits, and true human concierge escalations should cost 8 or more credits or be billed separately.

That leads to the most defensible first commercial structure. A $29 base plan should include something like 4 core credits per month, enough for real perceived value while leaving healthy margin on the baseline workflows. A $40 plan can either include more core credits or one bundled complex-task allowance. A $79 premium plan can include a small number of voice/provider credits and priority support. A $99 premium-plus plan should still meter or surcharge high-touch concierge instead of pretending it is free to deliver. The lowest price point, $20, works only if it is intentionally narrow: SMS/email coordination, low fan-out, no browser execution, no voice scheduling, and no included human rescue.

That recommendation is also consistent with the public family-assistant market floor. Consumer products like urlOhaiturn13search0 and urlGetherturn12search0 publicly anchor lower monthly prices, but those products are primarily centered on household organization, reminders, and shared scheduling rather than closed-loop execution through third-party contacts and providers. That makes Looheru’s promise more operationally expensive, but also more priceable if it is framed as “task completion” rather than “family planning software.” citeturn13search0turn13search1turn12search0turn12search2

My minimum-viable price recommendation is therefore:

  • If launch scope is only workflows one and two: $29/month.
  • If launch promise includes workflow three in a meaningful way: $40/month or $29 plus paid complex-task packs.
  • If launch promise includes workflow four as an included feature: $79/month with strict monthly provider-call credits.
  • Workflow five should not be packaged as unlimited monthly usage at any of these prices.

MVP instrumentation and cost logging

The MVP should instrument cost at the task level, not just the revenue level. The minimum useful event model is: task created, task classified, outreach enqueued, outreach sent, reply received, options compiled, parent prompted, parent selected, task confirmed, task failed, and task cancelled. Each event should carry workflow type, family ID, contact count, channel mix, elapsed time from creation, and whether human intervention occurred. The cost rollup should happen per task so that every completed task can produce a simple ledger row: telecom cost, email cost, AI/model cost, browser/tool cost, support minutes, and total variable cost.

For telecom and channel attribution, the most important things to log are segment count, direction, channel, provider status, and retry reason. For voice, log call minutes, answer rate, no-answer rate, voicemail/IVR outcomes, gather count or AI-agent runtime minutes, extraction success, and “slot confirmed” versus “slot offered only.” For model usage, log provider, model, input tokens, output tokens, web-search calls, browser minutes, and estimated cost in USD. For human work, log minutes, actor type, reason code, and outcome. Those few fields will tell you very quickly whether your margin problem is fan-out, retries, voice failures, or human cleanup. citeturn18search1turn19view0turn11search0turn7search0

The current repo should evolve toward a cost-ledger pattern rather than storing ever richer raw transcripts. Specifically, add a task_cost_ledger table with one row per costful action; add num_segments, encoding_class, template_key, and estimated_cost_usd fields around SMS sends; add provider_minutes, gather_uses, and ai_runtime_minutes to voice jobs; add an llm_usage table for tokens and tool calls; and add a human_interventions table for escalations. Use contact IDs instead of storing phone numbers or emails redundantly in analytics tables, and use reason codes rather than free-text notes wherever possible.

The privacy principle should be simple: store what you need to measure cost and reliability, not what you would be curious to read later. In practice that means keeping raw message bodies and transcripts out of cost analytics, hashing or ID-linking channel addresses, retaining only the minimum operational text needed for a short debug window, and moving long-term learning into structured metadata like outcome codes, slot counts, retry counts, and intervention reasons. Twilio’s own docs make this separation easier because message body is treated as PII while segment count is not. citeturn18search1

What most needs pilot data

The assumptions that matter most are not vendor prices. They are behavioral and operational. The highest-value pilot data points are: average contacts contacted per task; reply rate by channel; the percentage of tasks that need the next-day retry; average time-to-first-yes; completion rate by workflow; the share of tasks that require human intervention; the average human minutes by intervention reason; and the real-world provider-call success rate for clinics and offices. If those numbers are favorable, the business works. If they are not, no pricing page will save it.

The north-star metrics I would track from day one are completed tasks per paying family per month, time-to-confirm, cost per attempted task, cost per completed task, support minutes per completed task, and contribution margin per subscriber after payment fees. If I had to choose only one dashboard for the Friday discussion, it would be a cohort view of completed tasks, completed-task COGS, and human minutes per task by workflow. That dashboard will tell you whether Looheru is building software leverage or quietly building an underpriced concierge service.

Open questions and limitations

This is a strong first-pass model, but it is still a model. The two biggest unknowns are the actual completion rates in live family usage and the real percentage of tasks that require operator rescue. The browser/email and provider-voice workflows are especially sensitive to that second variable. I also used a simplified blended SMS carrier-fee assumption and a credit-card subscription-payment assumption; both are reasonable for planning, but they should be replaced with actual invoice data once the pilot starts. The most important practical next step is to instrument every task so the next version of this model can replace assumptions with real distributions.
