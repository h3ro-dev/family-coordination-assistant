Voice AI compliance and product-gating report for Looheru

Using the attached packet as the starting point, the highest-confidence position is to keep the founder-facing proposal aligned with the current strategy: sell the first paid MVP as saved-contact SMS and email coordination, while describing voice as a tightly designed, risk-gated capability that is limited to nonclinical logistics and only expands after counsel review, vendor diligence, and operational controls are in place.

Executive recommendation

Practical product recommendation. The safe proposal posture is: voice matters strategically, but autonomous healthcare voice should not be the headline promise of the first paid MVP. The first paid release can reasonably describe a limited, nonclinical coordination lane for voice if and only if it is scoped to publicly listed business main lines, uses immediate AI disclosure, avoids audio recording, avoids storing raw transcripts, suppresses calls to mobile/ambiguous numbers, supports office opt-out, and hard-stops when the conversation moves toward clinical, identity, insurance, medication, billing, or other sensitive topics. That posture is materially more defensible than promising provider-side or patient-clinical voice automation on day one. citeturn29view0turn19view0turn35view0turn11view0turn4search1

Legal facts. At the federal level, the entity["organization","Federal Communications Commission","U.S. communications regulator"] has already said that AI-generated human-sounding voices are “artificial or prerecorded voice” under the TCPA, so calling workflows that use AI voice inherit the same consent, identification, and opt-out risk analysis as other robocalls. The entity["organization","Federal Trade Commission","U.S. consumer protection agency"] treats telemarketing differently from informational calls, and it separately regulates certain non-HIPAA health apps through the FTC Act and the Health Breach Notification Rule. The entity["organization","U.S. Department of Health and Human Services","U.S. federal health agency"] draws a meaningful line between a consumer-side tool acting on the consumer’s behalf and a provider-side or plan-side tool acting on behalf of a covered entity or business associate. citeturn29view0turn19view0turn22view0turn20view0turn27view1turn24view1turn35view0

Assumptions. This recommendation assumes Looheru remains a direct-to-consumer family coordination tool in the MVP phase, does not operate as a provider workflow, does not accept provider-delegated patient management duties, does not market to provider offices during coordination calls, and does not claim to give medical, mental health, or insurance guidance. If any of those facts change, the risk analysis changes with them. citeturn35view0turn24view1turn25view0

MVP voice lane

Safe enough for MVP

Practical product recommendation. The safest MVP voice lane is: nonclinical outbound calls to verified business main lines for administrative coordination only. That lane can include asking whether a practice is accepting new patients, learning scheduling logistics, requesting the preferred scheduling path, confirming office hours, requesting forms or intake instructions, or asking where scheduling messages should be sent. It should exclude any discussion of symptoms, diagnoses, treatment choices, medications, insurance eligibility, prior authorization, or confidential teen/minor services. This is safer because federal robocall rules focus heavily on wireless/residential exposure and telemarketing content, while at least one high-relevance state statute—entity["state","California","U.S. state"]’s health AI disclosure law—expressly distinguishes “patient clinical information” from appointment scheduling, billing, and other clerical or business matters. citeturn19view0turn22view0turn20view0turn4search1

Specific MVP controls. The MVP lane is strongest if the system uses no audio recording, no voicemail drops, no retained raw transcripts, structured-only extraction, immediate AI disclosure at the start of the call, a human/off-ramp on request, and office opt-out suppression. Those controls do not eliminate risk, but they directly reduce the exposure created by state recording laws, AI disclosure statutes, and the broader FTC/HHS view that unnecessary retention and unauthorized disclosure of health-related data increase compliance and incident-response risk. citeturn8view0turn13view0turn11view0turn28view0turn27view1turn23view2

Proposed internal rule. “Safe enough” should mean safer, not risk-free. The rule should be: Voice may contact only a verified business landline or fixed business VoIP number, only for nonclinical logistics, only with upfront disclosure and opt-out, and only if the workflow can complete without collecting or retaining sensitive content. If the number is mobile, unclear, forwarded, or otherwise uncertain, the system should default to SMS, email, or human review instead of autonomous voice. citeturn19view0turn31view1

Human-reviewed lane

Practical product recommendation. The following categories should be human-reviewed before outbound contact and should usually require human takeover during the call: healthcare scheduling that is likely to involve patient-specific facts, therapy or behavioral health coordination, special-needs service coordination, identity or authority verification, and any interaction that may surface diagnoses, treatment plans, mental health details, medications, reproductive care, substance use information, or minor confidentiality questions. The reason is not that these topics are always unlawful for software to touch; it is that they sit on top of the most unstable mix of HIPAA boundary issues, minor-consent rules, state privacy doctrines, and heightened harm if the agent gets the context wrong. citeturn25view0turn25view1turn25view2turn11view0turn35view0

Legal facts. HHS states that whether a parent is the personal representative for a minor depends heavily on state law and on exceptions for services minors may obtain confidentially; the rule also permits providers to withhold parental access when disclosure is prohibited by state law or where safety concerns exist. HHS separately notes that providers must verify personal representative authority under 45 CFR 164.514(h). Those issues make identity and authority questions dangerous for a fully autonomous family-facing voice agent. citeturn25view0turn25view1turn25view2

Blocked lane

Practical product recommendation. The MVP should block, not merely “discourage,” the following voice uses: clinical triage; diagnosis or treatment discussion; medication questions; insurance, benefits, billing disputes, prior authorization, or claims advocacy; teen/minor confidential services; substance-use or therapy content; provider-side workflows carrying patient-specific clinical details; and any call that requires the agent to verify patient identity, personal-representative authority, or consent under provider rules. These are the categories most likely to drag the product from consumer coordination into regulated decision-making, sensitive-data processing, or provider-side business-associate territory. citeturn25view0turn25view2turn35view0turn24view1turn17view0

Federal issues

TCPA, prerecorded voice, and wireless consent

Legal facts. The FCC’s February 2024 Declaratory Ruling confirms that AI-generated human voices are covered by the TCPA’s “artificial or prerecorded voice” restrictions. Under 47 CFR 64.1200, non-emergency calls using an artificial or prerecorded voice to wireless numbers generally require prior express consent; telemarketing calls to those numbers require prior express written consent unless a specific exception applies. Residential prerecorded calls also have separate rules, with telemarketing treated more strictly and exempted informational or healthcare-related residential calls subject to numerical limits, identification requirements, and automated opt-out procedures. citeturn29view0turn19view0

Practical product recommendation. For MVP, the product should not rely on a theory that “provider office numbers are safe by default.” Instead it should verify line type before placement, suppress mobile and ambiguous numbers, avoid voicemail, and maintain request-based suppression lists. Many practice numbers forward to staff mobiles or mixed VoIP environments, and that creates avoidable TCPA risk. citeturn19view0turn31view1

FTC telemarketing versus informational calls

Legal facts. The Telemarketing Sales Rule defines telemarketing as a plan, program, or campaign conducted to induce the purchase of goods or services or a charitable contribution by telephone. FTC guidance also states that prerecorded “cold” sales calls are prohibited. That means the content and purpose of the call matter enormously: a call made to coordinate logistics is in a different bucket from a call made to market Looheru, sell a product, or persuade the recipient to buy or donate. citeturn22view0turn20view0

Practical product recommendation. The proposal should say that office calls are informational and coordination-oriented, not provider-marketing calls. The agent should never pitch Looheru, ask the office to buy anything, or upsell during the interaction. If the business goal ever changes from coordination to lead generation or provider sales, counsel should re-score the workflow as telemarketing before launch. citeturn22view0turn20view0

FTC consumer health data and the Health Breach Notification Rule

Legal facts. The FTC says many health apps and connected services that are not covered by HIPAA are still subject to Section 5 of the FTC Act and, where applicable, the Health Breach Notification Rule. In 2024 the FTC finalized amendments that expressly clarify the rule’s application to health apps and similar technologies not covered by HIPAA, and clarified that an unauthorized disclosure can itself be a reportable “breach of security.” FTC guidance further explains that a personal health record can exist where an app has the technical capacity to draw information from multiple sources and is managed or controlled primarily for the individual. citeturn27view1turn28view0turn23view0

Practical product recommendation. The MVP should be designed to stay out of the “consumer health record warehouse” pattern if possible. If the product starts storing longitudinal therapy/scheduling/condition data from multiple sources, the FTC health-app framework becomes more relevant even if HIPAA does not. Data minimization, deletion, and avoiding unauthorized disclosure are therefore not just good hygiene; they are meaningful scope controls. citeturn28view0turn27view1

HIPAA boundary

Legal facts. HHS guidance says a direct-to-consumer health app generally is not a business associate when it is acting on behalf of the consumer, even when the consumer imports data from her provider or directs the app to transmit data to a provider. By contrast, an app developer is acting as a business associate when it creates, receives, maintains, or transmits PHI on behalf of a covered entity—for example, where a provider contracts with the developer for patient management services, messaging, monitoring, or integrated provider workflows. HHS’s business-associate guidance similarly emphasizes that a covered entity may disclose PHI to a business associate only to help the covered entity carry out its own healthcare functions and must have a written BAA. citeturn35view0turn24view1turn24view2

Practical product recommendation. The proposal should say plainly that the paid MVP is a consumer-side coordination tool, not a provider workflow vendor. It can help a parent coordinate outreach, but it should not represent that Looheru is acting for the provider, the health plan, or the clinic in the MVP phase. The fastest way to create HIPAA business-associate exposure is to let the product manage provider-side scheduling or patient messaging on behalf of the office. citeturn35view0turn24view1turn24view2

State issues

AI disclosure

Legal facts. entity["state","Utah","U.S. state"]’s AI disclosure law requires disclosure in consumer transactions when a person asks whether AI is being used, and requires prominent disclosure when a person in a regulated occupation uses generative AI in a “high-risk artificial intelligence interaction,” including collection of health data and the provision of medical or mental-health advice. Utah also provides a safe harbor where the AI clearly and conspicuously discloses at the outset and throughout the interaction that it is generative AI or not human. entity["state","California","U.S. state"]’s AB 3030 separately requires covered health facilities, clinics, physician offices, and group practices using generative AI for communications about patient clinical information to disclose that fact and provide a human contact path, while expressly excluding scheduling, billing, and other clerical/business matters from “patient clinical information.” citeturn11view0turn4search1

Practical product recommendation. Even where a statute does not clearly require it, the MVP should use immediate AI disclosure as a universal product rule, because that is compatible with Utah’s safe-harbor logic, reduces deception risk, and creates cleaner evidence that the office was not misled. If Looheru ever deploys on the provider side in California for patient-clinical communications, it should assume AB 3030-style beginning-and-end disclosure and human-contact instructions are mandatory unless counsel says otherwise. citeturn11view0turn4search1

Recording, transcription, and STT

Legal facts. California’s Penal Code section 632 prohibits intentionally recording or eavesdropping on a confidential communication without the consent of all parties. Utah’s Interception of Communications Act generally allows one-party consent, and Colorado’s wiretapping/eavesdropping statutes similarly use an at-least-one-party consent model in core provisions. A respected secondary 50-state survey from the Reporters Committee notes that many state recording statutes are decades old and their application to modern communications can be unclear. citeturn8view0turn10view1turn7view0turn7view1turn13view0turn13view1

Practical product recommendation. For MVP, the best answer is not to debate whether live speech-to-text without saved audio “counts” as recording in every state. The best answer is to avoid audio recording entirely, avoid persistent raw transcripts, treat live ASR as legally sensitive, and get a 50-state counsel memo before saying the product can autonomously call nationwide at scale. This is one of the clearest places where product minimization beats legal bravado. citeturn8view0turn13view0

Utah, California, and Colorado relevance

Legal facts. Utah matters because it now has a direct AI disclosure regime. California matters because it is an all-party consent state for confidential call recording and has a healthcare AI disclosure statute that becomes relevant if Looheru ever moves into provider-side clinical communications; importantly, California’s statute excludes scheduling and other clerical/business matters from “patient clinical information.” Colorado matters because the state has enacted an AI law aimed at “high-risk” systems used in consequential decisions, including health-care services, although later legislation has pushed portions of the effective date to June 30, 2026; the law also excludes some natural-language technologies that merely provide information or recommendations unless they become a substantial factor in a consequential decision. citeturn8view0turn4search1turn17view0turn14search11

Practical product recommendation. Colorado should not stop a narrow scheduling MVP, but it is a warning light: if the product starts ranking providers, steering access, making authorization-like decisions, or materially affecting whether care is obtained or denied, Colorado-style “high-risk AI” arguments become much easier to make. Keep the MVP squarely in logistics and referrals, not access adjudication. citeturn17view0

What needs a 50-state counsel memo

Practical product recommendation. Outside counsel should deliver a short, launch-usable 50-state memo on five issues: recording/interception rules; mini-TCPA or state robocall statutes; state AI disclosure laws; state consumer-health-data/privacy statutes; and state rules on minors’ confidential services and parental access. Secondary surveys are useful for triage, but the launch memo should be keyed to Looheru’s exact call flow, line types, disclosures, retention model, and target states. citeturn13view0turn26search0turn26search12

Product requirements and engineering acceptance criteria

Product requirements

Practical product recommendation. The MVP voice feature should require all of the following before production launch:

  • Disclosure language at the start of every call. Example: “Hi, I’m Looheru’s automated assistant calling on behalf of a parent to help with scheduling and logistics. I’m not a clinician, and I won’t discuss treatment, medications, insurance, or other sensitive details. If you’d prefer, I can stop and route this to a human.” This aligns with the broadest low-friction disclosure posture and tracks the logic of Utah’s safe harbor and California’s provider-side AI disclosure norms. citeturn11view0turn4search1
  • Office opt-out with durable suppression. Example: “If your office does not want automated calls like this, please tell me now and we’ll mark that preference.” Federal prerecorded-call rules require opt-out mechanisms in many contexts and make suppression-list hygiene a normal part of compliant calling operations. citeturn19view0
  • No-audio-recording setting hard-disabled by default. Do not enable platform recording, dual-channel recording, voicemail capture, or “record for QA” toggles in the MVP. State recording exposure is avoidable here. citeturn8view0turn31view2
  • Transcript deletion and structured-only extraction. The system may transiently process speech for real-time understanding, but it should persist only a narrow structured output such as: office reached/not reached, scheduling route, callback number, extension, fax/email requested, intake link sent, next human step, and time windows. Raw transcript retention should be off by default, short-lived, and excluded from the permanent audit trail. This reduces health-data surface area under FTC/HHS frameworks. citeturn28view0turn27view1turn23view2
  • Line-type verification. Every dial target must be checked for mobile, landline, fixed VoIP, non-fixed VoIP, toll-free, or unknown status before voice placement. Unknown or mobile should block autonomous voice unless counsel approves a consent basis. citeturn31view1turn19view0
  • Human takeover triggers. Immediate transfer, pause, or escalation on detection of symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, medication, insurance, billing, prior authorization, date of birth, SSN, member ID, therapy content, minor confidentiality, anger/escalation, or identity-verification challenge. citeturn25view0turn25view1turn25view2turn35view0
  • Prohibited-data filters. Prompt-level and post-ASR filters should block creation or persistence of clinical narratives, mental-health details, minor-confidential-service details, or full insurance identifiers. citeturn25view2turn28view0
  • Audit log without sensitive content. The durable log should store metadata only: who initiated the call, which policy version ran, number classification, disclosure delivered yes/no, opt-out yes/no, blocked-trigger invoked yes/no, and the final structured outcome. Keep content out of the audit log unless counsel later signs off on a separate retention basis. citeturn23view2turn28view0

Engineering acceptance criteria

Practical product recommendation. A voice-safe MVP should not pass QA unless engineering can demonstrate all of the following:

  • The dialer refuses calls unless number type is verified and not mobile/unknown.
  • The call opens with a non-skippable disclosure and logs delivery.
  • Recording endpoints and recording webhooks are disabled in config and in the provider console.
  • Raw transcript persistence is disabled; only whitelisted structured fields can be saved.
  • The prohibited-topic classifier reliably halts or escalates on the blocked categories above.
  • The system offers office opt-out and writes suppression immediately.
  • Voicemail detection ends the session without leaving a message unless counsel later approves a separate voicemail policy.
  • The audit log excludes call content and sensitive identifiers.
  • Deletion jobs are tested and measurable.
  • Safety prompts and policies are versioned, testable, and locked for release.
  • Any provider-side integration, BAA-gated functionality, or PHI-bearing workflow is feature-flagged off in the MVP build. citeturn31view1turn31view2turn19view0turn35view0turn24view1

Vendor diligence

Communications, model, and email vendors

Practical product recommendation. The vendor review should be written as a gating checklist, not a procurement formality.

For urlTwilioturn31view0, confirm whether MVP uses only non-recording voice features, whether line-type verification via Lookup is mandatory in the outbound path, whether any subproducts used are HIPAA-eligible if scope later changes, and whether a BAA is needed or explicitly deferred for consumer-side non-provider workflows. Twilio publicly states that customers using its products for PHI workflows must execute a BAA, that only listed HIPAA-eligible products/services should be used for PHI workflows, and that Programmable Voice, SMS, and SIP can support HIPAA use cases under that structure. Twilio also documents line-type intelligence and separately warns that recording implicates consent laws. citeturn31view0turn31view1turn31view2turn31view3

For urlOpenAIturn33search4 or any other LLM provider, ask four concrete questions: whether business data is used to train models by default; what the default retention period is; whether zero-data-retention or comparable controls are available for the exact endpoints you plan to use; and whether a BAA is available if future provider-side or PHI scope emerges. OpenAI’s current business materials say it does not train on business data by default, that eligible customers can seek a BAA, and that the API platform includes configurable data-handling controls, including zero-data-retention availability for certain eligible use cases. citeturn32search15turn32search0turn33search0turn32search21

For urlResendturn34search0 or any email provider, ask whether the provider will sign a BAA, whether content is encrypted in transit and at rest, how long logs and backups are retained, whether message bodies appear in searchable support tooling, and whether the product should be treated as non-HIPAA by default. Proposal language and counsel questions

Proposal-ready language

Practical product recommendation. The official proposal can say this without sounding fearful:

Voice AI is strategically important because phone calls, hold times, and phone tag remain one of the hardest parts of family coordination. Our launch plan treats voice as a focused operational capability, not a blanket autonomy promise. In the first paid MVP, Looheru prioritizes saved-contact SMS and email coordination, while any voice capability is limited to nonclinical office logistics with clear AI disclosure, office opt-out, and human-review guardrails. We are intentionally designing voice to stay out of diagnosis, treatment, medications, insurance, and other sensitive workflows until counsel-reviewed controls and technical readiness support expansion.

That language is directionally accurate to the law and consistent with the current strategy packet because it emphasizes opportunity, preserves optionality, and explains the boundary as a product-quality decision rather than a retreat. citeturn29view0turn19view0turn35view0turn11view0turn4search1
